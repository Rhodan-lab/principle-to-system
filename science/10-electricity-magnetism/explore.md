# Exploring Electricity and Magnetism

## 1. Observation prompts

- **Static Cling:** On a dry day, rub a balloon against your hair or a wool sweater. Observe how it interacts with your hair, small pieces of paper, or a thin stream of water from a faucet. What happens to the interaction as the distance changes?
- **Magnetic Materials:** Take a small refrigerator magnet and test various objects around your home (coins, utensils, appliances, cans). Which materials are attracted to the magnet? Are all metals magnetic?
- **Appliance Cords:** With appliances unplugged and without opening or handling damaged cords, compare the external thickness markings of different power cords (e.g., a lamp vs. a high-power space heater or microwave). Notice the difference in the thickness of the wires. Why might a device that draws more current require a thicker wire?

## 2. Prediction questions

- If you break a bar magnet exactly in half, do you get one piece that is only a North pole and another that is only a South pole? Predict the magnetic properties of the two new pieces.
- Imagine two parallel wires carrying electric current in the same direction. Will they attract each other, repel each other, or have no effect on one another? What if the currents are in opposite directions?
- If you drop a strong magnet down a thick copper pipe (which is non-magnetic), will it fall at the same rate as a non-magnetic piece of metal of the same weight?

## 3. Worked reasoning examples

**Question:** Why does a bird sitting on a high-voltage power line not get electrocuted?

**Reasoning:**
1. **Identify the requirement for current flow:** For electric current to flow through an object, there must be a difference in electric potential (voltage) across that object, creating a closed circuit.
2. **Analyze the bird's situation:** The bird has both feet on the same wire. The wire is an excellent conductor, meaning there is very little resistance between the bird's two feet.
3. **Apply Ohm's Law ($V = IR$):** Because the resistance of the short segment of wire between the bird's feet is nearly zero, the voltage difference between its feet is also nearly zero.
4. **Compare paths:** Current divides among parallel paths according to their impedances. Because the bird's feet are nearly at the same potential, only a tiny voltage appears across its body under ordinary conditions.
5. **Conclusion:** Almost all the current continues to flow through the wire, and a negligible amount flows through the bird. However, if the bird were to touch a second wire at a different voltage, or a grounded pole, a large potential difference would exist across its body, resulting in electrocution.

## 4. Thought experiments

- **The Moving Charge:** Imagine you are running alongside an electron at the exact same velocity. To a stationary observer, the moving electron creates both an electric and a magnetic field. To you, the electron appears stationary. Do you measure a magnetic field? This thought experiment leads directly to the principles of special relativity.
- **The Infinite Wire:** Imagine an infinitely long, straight wire carrying a steady current. What is the shape of the magnetic field around it? How does the field strength change as you move further away?

## 5. Household and browser-based explorations

- **Electromagnet simulation:** Use a reputable virtual Faraday/electromagnet simulation to vary coil turns, current, and core material. Do not connect loose wire directly across a battery or household supply; a short circuit can overheat cells and conductors.
- **PhET Circuit Construction Kit:** Search online for the "PhET Circuit Construction Kit (DC)" simulation. Build virtual circuits with batteries, wires, resistors, and lightbulbs. Measure voltage and current with the virtual multimeters to verify Ohm's Law.
- **Faraday's Electromagnetic Lab:** Search for the "PhET Faraday's Electromagnetic Lab" simulation. Experiment with moving a magnet through a coil of wire to see how changing magnetic flux induces a voltage and lights a bulb.

## 6. Model-building prompts

- **Circuit Analogy:** Construct a conceptual model of a simple DC circuit using water flow as an analogy. What corresponds to the battery (voltage)? What corresponds to the wire? What corresponds to the current? What corresponds to a resistor? What are the limitations of this analogy?
- **Field Mapping:** Draw a model of the electric field lines around two positive charges placed near each other. Then, draw the field lines for one positive and one negative charge. Remember that field lines point in the direction of the force on a positive test charge and never cross.

## 7. Self-explanation questions

- Explain in your own words the difference between electric potential (voltage) and electric potential energy.
- Why is it necessary to have a *changing* magnetic field to induce a current, rather than just a strong, stationary magnetic field?
- How does a capacitor store energy differently than a battery?

## 8. Transfer questions

- The nervous system transmits signals using electrical impulses (action potentials). How do the concepts of voltage, current, and resistance apply to the flow of ions across a cell membrane?
- Earth has a magnetic field that protects us from solar wind. Based on what you know about the sources of magnetic fields, what must be happening inside the Earth to generate this field?
- How do wireless chargers for smartphones use the principles of electromagnetic induction?

## 9. Suggested learning paths

1. **Foundations:** Master the concepts of electric charge, Coulomb's Law, and the electric field.
2. **Current and Circuits:** Study Ohm's Law, series and parallel circuits, and Kirchhoff's laws.
3. **Magnetism:** Understand magnetic fields produced by permanent magnets and steady currents (Ampère's Law).
4. **Induction:** Explore Faraday's Law and Lenz's Law, understanding how changing fields interact.
5. **Synthesis:** Study Maxwell's equations conceptually to see the unified picture of electromagnetism and the nature of light.

## 10. Reasoning notes

When analyzing electromagnetic problems, always clearly define your system boundaries. Are you looking at the forces on a single charge, or the macroscopic behavior of a circuit? Pay close attention to vector directions—the right-hand rule is essential for determining the direction of magnetic fields and Lorentz forces. Remember that in circuit analysis, ideal wires are assumed to have zero resistance, meaning the entire wire is at the same electric potential.

## Phase 7 review boundaries and validity limits

- Electric and magnetic fields are components of one electromagnetic field whose decomposition depends on reference frame. They are not two unrelated substances.
- Ohm's law V = IR is a constitutive relation for approximately ohmic components under specified temperature and operating conditions, not a universal law for every device.
- Current divides among available branches according to circuit impedances and Kirchhoff's laws; it does not choose only a single “path of least resistance.”
- In the 2019 SI, c and e are exact defining constants, while μ₀ and ε₀ are experimentally determined quantities related through c² = 1/(μ₀ε₀).
- Lumped-circuit models are valid when propagation delays and distributed fields are negligible. At high frequency or large physical size, transmission-line and full-field models are required.
