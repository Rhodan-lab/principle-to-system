# Explore: Chemical Bonding and Reactions

## 1. Observation prompts
- Observe a glass of ice water on a humid day. Where does the condensation on the outside of the glass come from, and what intermolecular forces are responsible for its formation?
- Compare reliable reference data for sodium chloride and sucrose: melting or decomposition temperature, water solubility, and conductivity in solution. Which observations distinguish an ionic lattice from a molecular solid without heating substances at home?
- Notice how a drop of water beads up on a waxy leaf or a freshly waxed car, but spreads out flat on clean glass. What does this indicate about the polarity of the water molecules compared to the wax and the glass?

## 2. Prediction questions
- If you were to replace oxygen in water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) with the heavier group-16 element sulfur to form hydrogen sulfide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$), would you expect the boiling point to increase or decrease? Why?
- Consider a reaction where a solid piece of metal reacts with a gas. If otherwise identical samples have smaller particle size and therefore greater exposed area, how will the reaction rate change? What kinetic principle explains this?
- If a reversible exothermic reaction is at equilibrium, and you suddenly increase the temperature of the system, in which direction will the equilibrium shift to compensate?

## 3. Worked reasoning examples
**Question:** Why does ice float on liquid water, whereas most solid substances sink in their liquid forms?
**Reasoning:**
1. **Identify the relevant forces:** Water molecules interact via hydrogen bonding, a strong dipole-dipole interaction.
2. **Analyze the liquid state:** In liquid water, molecules have enough kinetic energy to constantly break and reform hydrogen bonds, allowing them to pack relatively closely together.
3. **Analyze the solid state:** As water cools and freezes, the kinetic energy decreases. The molecules arrange themselves into a rigid, hexagonal crystalline lattice to maximize the number of stable hydrogen bonds (four per molecule).
4. **Compare densities:** This hexagonal lattice structure is highly open and porous compared to the disordered arrangement in the liquid. Because the molecules are held further apart in the solid state, ice has a lower density than liquid water.
5. **Conclusion:** Since less dense objects float in denser fluids, ice floats on water.

## 4. Thought experiments
Imagine you have a magical pair of tweezers that allows you to pull individual molecules apart. You grab the two hydrogen atoms in a water molecule and slowly pull them away from the central oxygen atom. 
- What happens to the electron density as you pull? 
- At what point does the covalent bond "break"? 
- Once broken, do the hydrogen atoms leave with their shared electrons, or does the more electronegative oxygen keep them? This thought experiment explores the concept of bond dissociation energy and heterolytic versus homolytic cleavage.

## 5. Household and browser-based explorations
- **Solubility and Polarity:** Mix water and cooking oil in a clear glass. Observe how they separate. Now add a few drops of dish soap and stir vigorously. Observe the result. The soap molecules have a polar "head" and a nonpolar "tail," allowing them to bridge the gap between the polar water and nonpolar oil, forming an emulsion.
- **Reaction-rate simulation:** Use a browser-based collision or reaction-rate simulation to compare particle concentration, temperature, surface area, and catalyst pathways. Record which variable changes collision frequency and which changes the energy barrier.
- **Browser Exploration:** Search for an interactive PhET simulation on "Molecule Shapes" or "VSEPR Theory." Build various molecules by adding bonding pairs and lone pairs to a central atom, and observe how the 3D geometry changes to minimize repulsion.

## 6. Model-building prompts
- Construct a physical model of a methane ($\text{CH}_4$) molecule using marshmallows (or clay) and toothpicks. Try to arrange the four hydrogen atoms so they are as far apart from each other as possible. Measure the angles between the toothpicks. You have just modeled a tetrahedral geometry.
- Draw a causal loop diagram showing the relationship between temperature, kinetic energy, collision frequency, and reaction rate.

## 7. Self-explanation questions
- Explain in your own words why a catalyst increases the rate of a reaction but does not change the final equilibrium concentrations.
- Why is it that carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$) has polar bonds but is an overall nonpolar molecule, whereas water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) has polar bonds and is a polar molecule?
- How does the concept of electronegativity help predict whether a bond will be ionic, polar covalent, or nonpolar covalent?

## 8. Transfer questions
- Catalytic converters must reach useful conversion quickly after a cold start. Which kinetic, heat-transfer, and surface-area variables influence light-off without changing the equilibrium constant?
- How might the principles of intermolecular forces be applied by pharmaceutical researchers designing a new drug that needs to bind specifically to a certain protein receptor in the body?

## 10. Reasoning notes
When predicting molecular geometry, always start with a correct Lewis structure. Failing to account for lone pairs on the central atom is the most common error in VSEPR predictions. Remember that lone pairs exert a greater repulsive force than bonding pairs, which slightly compresses the angles between the bonded atoms. Furthermore, when considering intermolecular forces, size matters; larger molecules have more electrons, leading to stronger London dispersion forces, which can sometimes outweigh the effects of dipole-dipole interactions in smaller molecules.

## Phase 7 review boundaries and validity limits

- Ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding are useful limiting descriptions of electron density and many-body interactions, not perfectly separate boxes. “Full shells” are a heuristic rather than a universal cause of bonding.
- VSEPR predicts many main-group molecular shapes qualitatively but does not replace quantum chemistry and is unreliable for several transition-metal, delocalised, or hypervalent systems.
- Equilibrium constants are dimensionless when written using activities relative to standard states. Concentrations and partial pressures are approximations whose validity depends on non-ideality.
- A catalyst changes the reaction mechanism and rate constants but not the equilibrium constant or thermodynamic state difference. It may change chemical form during a catalytic cycle and is regenerated overall rather than literally remaining unchanged at every instant.
- Bond breaking requires energy, while the net energy of a reaction depends on all bonds and interactions broken and formed plus environmental conditions.
