# Cells, membranes, enzymes, metabolism, and bioenergetics

## 1. Observation prompts

- Observe a slice of raw potato or apple left exposed to the air. Note the browning process over time. This is an enzyme-catalyzed reaction (polyphenol oxidase). How does the rate of browning change if you coat the slice in lemon juice (acidic) or place it in the refrigerator?
- Place equal drops of food colouring into room-temperature water and safely warmed water prepared by an adult or teacher; do not handle near-boiling water. Observe the rate of diffusion. How does temperature affect the kinetic energy of the molecules and their movement?
- Look at a wilted houseplant. After watering it, observe how long it takes for the leaves to become firm (turgid) again. This demonstrates osmosis and the role of the plant cell membrane and vacuole in maintaining turgor pressure.

## 2. Prediction questions

- If a cell is placed in a hypertonic solution (a solution with a higher solute concentration than the cell's interior), predict the net direction of water movement. What will happen to the cell's volume?
- Consider an enzyme-catalyzed reaction operating at $V_{\max}$. If you double the substrate concentration, predict the effect on the reaction rate. What if you double the enzyme concentration instead?
- Predict the consequence on ATP production if a chemical compound makes the inner mitochondrial membrane highly permeable to protons, effectively short-circuiting the proton motive force.

## 3. Worked reasoning examples

**Scenario:** A researcher is studying a new inhibitor for a specific enzyme. They find that adding the inhibitor increases the apparent $K_M$ of the enzyme but does not change the $V_{\max}$. What type of inhibition is this, and how does it work?

**Reasoning:**
1. **Identify the parameters:** $V_{\max}$ is the maximum rate when all enzyme active sites are saturated with substrate. $K_M$ is the substrate concentration giving half of $V_{\max}$ in the simple model; it is not automatically a direct affinity measurement.
2. **Analyze the changes:** The fact that $V_{\max}$ is unchanged means that at very high substrate concentrations, the enzyme can still achieve its maximum catalytic rate. This implies the substrate can outcompete the inhibitor.
3. **Analyze the $K_M$ increase:** An increased apparent $K_M$ means more substrate is needed to reach half-maximal rate under these conditions; the parameter shift is consistent with the competitive-inhibition model without making $K_M$ a universal binding constant.
4. **Conclusion:** This is characteristic of **competitive inhibition**. The inhibitor binds to the active site, competing directly with the substrate. High concentrations of substrate can displace the inhibitor, allowing the reaction to reach $V_{\max}$, but the presence of the inhibitor means more substrate is required to achieve half-maximal velocity.

## 4. Thought experiments

- **The Maxwell's Demon of the Cell:** Imagine a microscopic entity (a "demon") guarding a pore in a cell membrane. It only allows fast-moving (hot) molecules to exit and slow-moving (cold) molecules to enter. Over time, the cell would cool down while the environment heats up, seemingly violating the Second Law of Thermodynamics. How do actual membrane transport proteins avoid this paradox? (Hint: Consider the energy required to operate the "door" and the role of ATP).
- **The Infinite Enzyme:** Suppose an enzyme could be engineered to have a $K_M$ of zero and an infinite turnover number ($k_{cat}$). What physical constraints would prevent this enzyme from actually catalyzing reactions at an infinite rate in a real cellular environment? Consider the role of diffusion and the viscosity of the cytoplasm.

## 5. Household and browser-based explorations

- **Potato-strip osmosis:** Cut equal potato strips with adult or teacher supervision. Place them in labelled cups containing water and pre-prepared salt solutions, then measure mass or length changes. Do not taste laboratory samples. Compare observations with water-potential and membrane models rather than saying water moves to ‘balance solute concentrations.’
- **Yeast gas-production observation:** Use a small, flexible balloon or a loosely covered cup rather than a rigid sealed vessel. Compare room-temperature mixtures with different sugar conditions, keep quantities small, and stop if pressure or foaming becomes excessive. Gas production is evidence of metabolism but does not by itself measure ATP yield.
- **Browser exploration:** Use an institutional animation or simulation of oxidative phosphorylation. Track electron transfer, proton pumping, membrane potential, ATP synthase, oxygen consumption, and heat separately; identify which variables the model omits.

## 6. Model-building prompts

- **Constructing a Membrane:** Using craft materials (e.g., cotton swabs for lipids, clay for proteins), build a model of the fluid mosaic membrane. Ensure you represent the hydrophobic tails and hydrophilic heads of the phospholipids. Add different types of proteins to represent channels, carriers, and receptors.
- **Modeling Enzyme Kinetics:** Create a physical or computational model (using a spreadsheet) to simulate Michaelis-Menten kinetics. Use a fixed number of "enzymes" (e.g., people) and a variable number of "substrates" (e.g., objects to be moved from one table to another). Record the rate of transfer as you increase the number of objects, and plot the data to find $V_{\max}$ and $K_M$.

## 7. Self-explanation questions

- Explain in your own words why the hydrolysis of ATP releases energy, avoiding the phrase "breaking a high-energy bond." Focus on the stability of the reactants versus the products.
- Describe the journey of a carbon atom from a molecule of glucose through glycolysis and the citric acid cycle until it is exhaled as carbon dioxide.
- How does the structure of the mitochondrion (specifically the highly folded inner membrane) relate to its function in oxidative phosphorylation?

## 8. Transfer questions

- In a model, block electron transfer to the terminal acceptor without naming or handling any real inhibitor. Predict changes in proton pumping, membrane potential, oxygen consumption, NADH oxidation, ATP synthesis, and heat production. Which predictions depend on cell type and timescale?
- In industrial biotechnology, how would changing enzyme expression alter flux, burden, redox balance, growth, byproducts, and stability rather than simply ‘maximising one pathway’?
- How do electrochemical gradients and selective channels contribute to an action potential, and why is a membrane voltage not itself a flow of electrical current?

## 10. Reasoning notes

When analyzing bioenergetic systems, always trace the flow of electrons and protons. The energy derived from the oxidation of food is fundamentally about stripping high-energy electrons from carbon compounds and passing them down an energy gradient. The coupling of this electron flow to the pumping of protons creates the electrochemical gradient that ultimately drives ATP synthesis. Remember that enzymes do not change the thermodynamics of these reactions; they only provide a lower-energy pathway for the reactions to occur at a rate compatible with life. Membrane transport is the physical manifestation of these thermodynamic principles, allowing cells to maintain the non-equilibrium state that defines living systems.
