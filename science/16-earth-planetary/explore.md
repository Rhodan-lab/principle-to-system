# Earth, Atmosphere, Oceans, Climate, and Planetary Systems

## 1. Observation prompts
- Look at a world map or globe. Notice how the eastern coastline of South America seems to fit into the western coastline of Africa. What does this suggest about the history of these landmasses?
- Observe the clouds on a windy day. Do they all move in the same direction, or do clouds at different altitudes move differently? What does this tell you about atmospheric circulation?
- If you live near a coast, observe the tides over a few days. How does the water level change? How does this relate to the position of the moon?
- Look at the shape of a river valley versus a valley carved by a glacier (if you have access to images or live near one). How does the flow of water versus solid ice shape the Earth differently?

## 2. Prediction questions
- If the Earth's rotation were to suddenly stop, how would the global wind patterns change? What would happen to the Coriolis effect?
- If a massive volcanic eruption injected millions of tons of sulfur dioxide aerosols into the stratosphere, what would be the short-term effect on global temperatures? Why?
- If the Greenland ice sheet were to melt completely, adding a massive influx of fresh water to the North Atlantic, how might this affect the thermohaline circulation?
- If the concentration of atmospheric $CO_2$ doubles, how will the radiative forcing change, assuming the logarithmic relationship holds?

## 3. Worked reasoning examples
**Question:** Why is the equator warmer than the poles?
**Reasoning:** 
1. The Earth is a sphere illuminated by a distant source (the Sun).
2. At the equator, sunlight strikes the surface almost perpendicularly (at a 90-degree angle). The solar energy is concentrated over a small area.
3. At the poles, sunlight strikes the surface at a shallow angle. The same amount of solar energy is spread over a much larger area.
4. Furthermore, sunlight at the poles must travel through a thicker layer of the atmosphere, leading to more scattering and absorption before reaching the surface.
5. Finally, the poles are covered in ice and snow, which have a high albedo, reflecting a large portion of the incoming solar radiation back into space.
6. Therefore, the equator absorbs significantly more solar energy per unit area than the poles, resulting in higher temperatures.

## 4. Thought experiments
- **The Snowball Earth:** Imagine a scenario where a slight cooling trend causes ice caps to grow. Ice reflects more sunlight than ocean or land (higher albedo). This reflection causes further cooling, leading to more ice growth. Follow this positive feedback loop to its logical conclusion. How could the Earth ever escape this "Snowball" state? (Hint: Think about the carbon cycle and volcanoes).
- **The Hollow Earth:** Imagine the Earth was hollow, with all its mass concentrated in a thin outer shell. How would gravity on the surface differ? Would plate tectonics still be possible? Why or why not?

## 5. Household and browser-based explorations

- **Convection simulation:** Use a browser-based fluid or mantle-convection simulation rather than heating a dish on a stove. Change viscosity, heating location, boundary conditions, and density contrast; explain why a low-viscosity water model does not reproduce solid-mantle rheology.
- **Rotation on a sphere:** Use a digital globe or removable tape on a ball rather than a permanent marker. Compare a straight path in an inertial frame with its apparent deflection in a rotating frame.
- **Earth-data exploration:** Use an institutional wind, ocean, earthquake, or climate-data viewer. Record variable definitions, units, timestamps, coverage gaps, and whether the display shows observations, reanalysis, or model output.

## 6. Model-building prompts
- **Energy Balance Model:** Using a spreadsheet, build a simple zero-dimensional energy balance model. Input the solar constant and Earth's albedo to calculate the effective emission temperature. Then, add a parameter for the greenhouse effect (emissivity) to see how changing it affects the surface temperature.
- **Carbon Cycle Box Model:** Draw a diagram representing the major carbon reservoirs (atmosphere, ocean, land biosphere, fossil fuels). Add arrows representing the fluxes between them. Assign rough values to the fluxes (e.g., in Gigatons of Carbon per year). What happens to the atmospheric reservoir if you increase the flux from fossil fuels?

## 7. Self-explanation questions
- Explain in your own words how the greenhouse effect works, distinguishing between shortwave and longwave radiation.
- Why does oceanic crust subduct beneath continental crust, rather than the other way around?
- How does the density of seawater change with temperature and salinity, and why is this important for global ocean circulation?
- What is the difference between weather and climate?

## 8. Transfer questions

- How would rotation rate, planetary radius, atmospheric depth, heating pattern, and friction jointly affect circulation on another planet? Avoid attributing every difference to Coriolis effects alone.
- Compare hypothetical changes in atmospheric composition using a radiative-transfer model. Why does increasing a greenhouse gas not translate directly into a fixed surface-temperature change without feedbacks and ocean heat uptake?
- For a rocky exoplanet, list the unknown material properties and boundary conditions required before inferring plate tectonics from planet mass alone.

## 9. Suggested learning paths
- **To understand the deep Earth:** Start with mineralogy and rock types, move to seismology (how we know what's inside), then study mantle convection and plate tectonics.
- **To understand climate change:** Start with the Earth's energy budget and radiative forcing, study the carbon cycle, then explore the history of Earth's climate (paleoclimatology) before looking at modern climate models.
- **To understand ocean dynamics:** Start with the physical properties of seawater, study surface currents and wind stress, then explore deep-water formation and thermohaline circulation.

## 10. Reasoning notes
When reasoning about the Earth system, always consider the **scale** of the process. A mechanism that is dominant on a local scale (like a sea breeze) may be negligible on a global scale. Pay attention to **feedbacks**. The Earth system is full of positive feedbacks (which amplify changes, like the ice-albedo feedback) and negative feedbacks (which dampen changes, like the weathering thermostat). Understanding these feedbacks is crucial for predicting how the system will respond to perturbations. Finally, remember that the Earth is a **closed system** for matter (mostly) but an **open system** for energy. Mass is conserved and cycled, while energy flows through.
