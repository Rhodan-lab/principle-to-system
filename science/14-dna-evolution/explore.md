## 1. Observation prompts

- Use an anonymised or fictional pedigree containing clearly defined single-gene traits. Which inheritance patterns are consistent with the data, and which alternatives remain possible? Avoid inferring sensitive family or health information from appearance.
- Observe visible variation in a common local plant or animal population without touching, feeding, or disturbing organisms. Which differences are measurable, and which environmental variables could also explain them?
- Compare published diagrams or museum images of homologous structures. Separate similarity due to common ancestry from similarity caused by convergent function.

## 2. Prediction questions

- In a digital population model, suppose one heritable variant has higher reproductive success under a changed environment. Predict allele-frequency trajectories while varying population size, dominance, migration, and drift.
- A promoter mutation changes transcription-factor or polymerase occupancy. Why can expression increase, decrease, remain unchanged, or become condition-dependent rather than following a single ‘tighter binding means more expression’ rule?
- If a small group colonises an isolated habitat, how might sampling, drift, inbreeding, migration, and later selection alter diversity relative to the source population?

## 3. Worked reasoning examples

**Scenario:** A researcher is studying a gene that codes for a crucial metabolic enzyme. They discover a point mutation (a change in a single nucleotide) in the coding region of the gene. However, when they analyze the protein produced by the mutated gene, they find its amino acid sequence is identical to the wild-type (normal) protein.

**Reasoning:**
1.  **Identify the core mechanism:** The translation of mRNA into protein relies on the genetic code, where triplets of nucleotides (codons) specify amino acids.
2.  **Recall the properties of the genetic code:** The genetic code is *degenerate* or *redundant*, meaning that most amino acids are specified by more than one codon. For example, the codons CCU, CCC, CCA, and CCG all code for the amino acid proline [1].
3.  **Apply to the scenario:** The point mutation changed the nucleotide sequence of the mRNA codon. However, because of the redundancy of the genetic code, the new mutated codon happens to specify the exact same amino acid as the original codon.
4.  **Conclusion:** This is a synonymous substitution: the encoded amino-acid sequence is unchanged. It may still affect RNA structure, splicing, translation rate, expression, or fitness, so unchanged protein sequence does not guarantee no phenotypic effect.

## 4. Thought experiments

*   **The Infinite Population:** Imagine a population of organisms that is infinitely large, mates completely randomly, experiences no mutations, has no migration in or out, and where all individuals have equal reproductive success. According to the Hardy-Weinberg principle, what happens to the genetic variation in this population over time? Why is this scenario impossible in the real world, and what does this tell us about the inevitability of evolution?
*   **The Universal Translator:** Suppose you could engineer a ribosome that could translate any sequence of DNA directly into a protein, bypassing the need for transcription into mRNA. What regulatory mechanisms would the cell lose? How would this affect the cell's ability to respond to environmental changes?

## 5. Household and browser-based explorations

- **Genome-browser exploration:** Use an official genome browser or NCBI sequence record for a non-sensitive example gene, such as a plant pigment or bacterial metabolic gene. Identify exons or coding regions, strand direction, transcripts, and evidence annotations. Do not treat a database label as proof of function without checking evidence.
- **Population-genetics simulation:** Use a browser simulator or spreadsheet to compare drift, migration, selection, and mutation. Run repeated trials; a single stochastic trajectory is not a general result.
- **Replication-fidelity model:** Simulate copying a symbolic sequence with a defined error probability, then add proofreading and repair stages. Compare per-copy error, total errors, and the distribution across many trials.

## 6. Model-building prompts

*   Using physical materials (like colored beads, wire, or building blocks), construct a model of a short segment of a DNA double helix. Ensure your model accurately represents the sugar-phosphate backbone, the antiparallel nature of the strands, and the specific pairing of adenine with thymine and cytosine with guanine.
*   Create a flowchart or diagram that traces the flow of information from a specific sequence of DNA nucleotides, through transcription to mRNA, and finally to the translation of a short polypeptide chain. Include the roles of RNA polymerase, ribosomes, and tRNA.

## 7. Self-explanation questions

*   Explain why DNA replication is described as "semi-conservative." What is the alternative, and how did the Meselson-Stahl experiment prove the semi-conservative model?
*   Describe the difference between transcription and translation. Where does each process occur within a eukaryotic cell?
*   How does genetic drift differ from natural selection? In what types of populations is genetic drift most impactful?

## 8. Transfer questions

*   The principles of natural selection apply to biological organisms. Can you apply these same principles to explain the evolution of non-biological systems, such as human languages, technological innovations, or computer algorithms?
*   How does understanding the mechanisms of viral mutation and evolution inform the design of public health strategies, such as the development of annual influenza vaccines?

## 9. Suggested learning paths

*   **Path 1: The Molecular Machinery:** Focus on the physical and chemical processes of the central dogma. Study the detailed mechanisms of DNA replication, the enzymes involved in transcription, and the complex structure of the ribosome during translation.
*   **Path 2: Patterns of Inheritance:** Explore Mendelian genetics in depth. Learn how to use Punnett squares to predict the outcomes of monohybrid and dihybrid crosses, and investigate non-Mendelian inheritance patterns like incomplete dominance and polygenic traits.
*   **Path 3: Population Dynamics:** Delve into the mathematics of evolution. Study the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, the effects of genetic drift and gene flow, and the different modes of natural selection (directional, stabilizing, and disruptive).

## 10. Reasoning notes

When reasoning about genetics and evolution, it is crucial to distinguish between the individual level and the population level. Mutations occur in individuals, but evolution is a process that occurs in populations over generations. Furthermore, remember that natural selection acts on phenotypes, not directly on genotypes. A recessive allele that causes a severe disease can remain hidden in a population within heterozygous carriers, shielded from the immediate effects of natural selection. Finally, avoid teleological thinking; evolution does not have a goal or a predetermined direction. Adaptations arise because they confer a reproductive advantage in a specific, current environment, not because an organism "needs" them to survive in the future.
