## 1. Observation prompts

- Look at a neon sign or a sodium street lamp. Notice the distinct, pure colour of the light compared to the broad spectrum of an incandescent bulb or the sun. What does this purity suggest about the energy transitions happening inside the gas atoms?
- Observe the reflection of light off a CD or DVD. The rainbow pattern is caused by diffraction, a wave property of light. How does this macroscopic wave behaviour reconcile with the idea that light is composed of individual photon particles?
- Consider a solid object, like a wooden desk or a metal spoon. If atoms are mostly empty space (a tiny nucleus surrounded by a probability cloud of electrons), why does the object feel solid? Why doesn't your hand pass through it?

## 2. Prediction questions

- If you were to double the frequency of light shining on a metal surface in a photoelectric effect experiment, what would happen to the kinetic energy of the ejected electrons? What would happen to the number of electrons ejected per second?
- Based on the Pauli exclusion principle, predict the maximum number of electrons that can occupy the $n=3$ principal energy level.
- If an electron is confined to a smaller and smaller region of space (decreasing $\Delta x$), what must happen to its momentum according to the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle?

## 3. Worked reasoning examples

**Question:** Why do different elements emit different colours of light when heated?

**Reasoning:**
1. **Identify the system:** We are looking at atoms of a specific element being subjected to thermal energy.
2. **Apply quantum principles:** Electrons in these atoms occupy specific, quantised energy levels.
3. **Trace the causal chain (Excitation):** The thermal energy causes electrons to jump from their ground state to higher, excited energy levels.
4. **Trace the causal chain (Emission):** These excited states are unstable. The electrons quickly drop back down to lower energy levels.
5. **Connect to observables:** As an electron drops to a lower level, it must release the energy difference. It does this by emitting a photon. The energy of the photon ($E$) is exactly equal to the difference in energy levels ($\Delta E$).
6. **Synthesise:** Because $E = hc/\lambda$, a specific energy difference corresponds to a specific wavelength (colour) of light. Since every element has a unique nuclear charge and electron configuration, every element has a unique set of energy levels. Therefore, the possible energy differences ($\Delta E$) are unique to each element, resulting in a unique spectrum of emitted colours.

## 4. Thought experiments

- **Schrödinger's Cat:** Imagine a cat in a sealed box with a radioactive atom, a Geiger counter, and a vial of poison gas. If the atom decays, the counter triggers the release of the gas, killing the cat. If it doesn't decay, the cat lives. According to the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics, until the box is opened and observed, the atom is in a superposition of decayed and undecayed states. Does this mean the macroscopic cat is simultaneously alive and dead? This thought experiment highlights the conceptual difficulties of applying quantum superposition to macroscopic objects and the problem of measurement.
- **The Double-Slit Experiment with Single Electrons:** Imagine firing electrons one by one at a barrier with two narrow slits, with a detector screen behind it. Even though the electrons are fired individually (as particles), over time, an interference pattern builds up on the screen, characteristic of waves. If you place a detector at the slits to see *which* slit the electron goes through, the interference pattern disappears, and you get a simple particle pattern. How does the act of measurement destroy the wave-like behaviour?

## 5. Household and browser-based explorations

- **Browser Exploration (PhET Interactive Simulations):** Search for the "Models of the Hydrogen Atom" simulation by PhET. Switch between the classical solar system model, the Bohr model, and the Schrödinger model. Observe how the Schrödinger model represents the electron not as a dot, but as a probability density cloud.
- **Household Exploration (Spectroscopy):** If you have a diffraction grating (often sold cheaply online or found in some educational kits), look at different light sources through it: an incandescent bulb, a fluorescent tube, an LED, and a neon sign. Compare the continuous spectrum of the hot filament with the discrete emission lines of the gases.

## 6. Model-building prompts

- Construct a visual model (using drawing software or physical materials) comparing the Bohr model of the atom with the quantum mechanical model. Highlight the difference between a defined orbit and a probability orbital.
- Create a flowchart mapping the causal chain of the photoelectric effect, starting from the incident photon to the ejected electron, explicitly noting the threshold frequency requirement.

## 7. Self-explanation questions

- Explain in your own words why the concept of a "trajectory" (a defined path through space) is meaningless for an electron in an atom.
- How does the Pauli exclusion principle prevent all the electrons in a heavy atom from simply falling into the lowest energy $1s$ orbital?
- Why is the wave nature of matter obvious for an electron but completely undetectable for a thrown baseball?

## 8. Transfer questions

- The principles of quantum mechanics govern the behaviour of electrons in atoms. How might these same principles apply to the behaviour of protons and neutrons inside the atomic nucleus?
- If you could somehow alter the value of Planck's constant ($h$) to be much larger, how would the macroscopic world change? Would quantum effects become visible to the naked eye?

## 10. Reasoning notes

When reasoning about quantum systems, it is crucial to abandon classical intuition. Do not try to picture an electron as a tiny billiard ball. Instead, rely on the mathematical models and the principles of probability. Remember that in the quantum realm, properties like position and momentum are not definite until they are measured, and the act of measurement inherently disturbs the system. Always ask: "What is the quantised property here?" and "What are the allowed states?"
